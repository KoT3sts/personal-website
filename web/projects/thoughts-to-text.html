Thoughts-to-Text BCI

From brain signals to letters.

In 30 seconds

Problem
Turn the EEG of attempted handwriting into text, one character at a time, to help people who can no longer write.
My part
As lead researcher, I worked on decoding attempted handwriting from non-invasive EEG, one character at a time, with an autoencoder and t-SNE. I presented the work at NCUR, the National Conference on Undergraduate Research, and in two KSU symposium presentations in 2022, plus a related motor-imagery talk. I’m the first presenter listed on the Fall 2022 talk and the only presenter on the Spring 2022 poster.
Result
A method presented in 2022: an autoencoder compresses the EEG into a low-dimensional space, and t-SNE maps it to 2D to show how the characters separate.
Proof
  • 2 KSU symposium presentations, 2022, and a related talk
  • Presented at NCUR and received a $2,000 Research Excellence stipend.

What the study did

Earlier systems had people choose from a small, fixed set of words. This study aimed for a model that could type any word in English, so participants attempted to handwrite each character one at a time, following instructions on a computer screen.

The EEG was hard to work with: it is noisy, and trials of different characters look alike. To see whether the characters separate at all, an autoencoder compresses the data into a low-dimensional space, and t-SNE maps that space onto a 2D plane.

One hard decision

Earlier studies had people use their neural signals to pick from a limited set of words. We chose the more general design: decode one character at a time, so the model could type any word in English, not only the words on a list.

One limit

EEG is noisy, and trials of different characters look alike, which makes the data hard to process. The presented work maps how the characters separate; it is not a finished real-time typing system.
